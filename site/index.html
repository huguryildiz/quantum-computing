Quantum computing, one idea at a time

An interactive course in the software half of quantum computing: what a quantum state is, what can be done to it, what a measurement returns, and what an algorithm has to arrange before that measurement is worth making.

Written for students who have had linear algebra and some Python, and no quantum mechanics. Every scene says what is going on in ordinary words before it says it in symbols. The whole course is one file and runs with no network.

What the course is built to install

Three sentences, and every chapter answers to them

A student usually arrives believing that a quantum computer tries every answer at once. Every later idea is harder to read while that sentence is still believed, so the course replaces it with three that are true.

ONE

The state is large, the readout is small

A measurement of n qubits returns n bits, never all 2n amplitudes. An algorithm earns nothing by holding many amplitudes unless it can make the unwanted ones cancel first.

TWO

A global phase is nothing, a relative phase is everything

Interference is the only mechanism any algorithm in this course has, and it is entirely a statement about the phase between two terms of one state.

THREE

A resource claim names five things

The task, the input model, the accuracy, the hardware model, and the classical baseline. A claim missing one of the five is not yet a claim, and a query count is not a runtime.

The sequence

Seven chapters, ending where the next can start

Each chapter carries its own laboratories, where a control changes an equation rather than a picture, and twenty questions with the error each one is built to catch said plainly.

00

The frame of the course

What a quantum computer is for, how large the state really is, and the one interference experiment the rest of the course is a generalisation of.

01

The mathematics of quantum states

States as complex columns, the inner product and what it conjugates, amplitude against phase, projectors, the tensor product, and the operators a measurement and a gate turn out to be.

02

States, measurement and dynamics

The Born rule, what a measurement leaves behind, observables and their spreads, the Pauli algebra, evolution as an exponential, and what a finite run of shots is worth.

03

Mixed states and entanglement

The density operator and the two situations no state vector describes; channels, relaxation and dephasing; the partial trace; the Bell states, and the correlation that no model with pre-existing values can produce.

04

The Bloch sphere and quantum gates

One qubit drawn, and every operation on it as a motion of that drawing. The half angle, the Pauli gates, the Hadamard, the phase family, and the two-qubit ordering that fails silently.

05

Circuits and protocols

The machine as it is actually run: depth against gate count, shots and error bars, what a compiler does before the hardware sees anything. Then teleportation and Grover, worked end to end.

06

Quantum algorithms

One mechanism — phase kickback — and the four algorithms built from it: Deutsch–Jozsa, the quantum Fourier transform, phase estimation, and order finding with the factoring that follows from it.

Fixed everywhere, without local variation

Four conventions, and two of them fail silently

Most of the errors that survive a whole calculation come from mixing two conventions in one line. These are stated once and never varied.

Qubit order

|qₙ₋₁ … q₁q₀⟩

Entry x of the column of amplitudes is the amplitude of the basis state x, read as a binary number. Getting this wrong does not make a result approximately wrong; it names a different state, and every number after it is quietly wrong.

Phase

eiγ|ψ⟩ ≡ |ψ⟩

A phase on the whole state may always be dropped. A phase between two terms may never be dropped, and it is the only thing interference is made of.

The inner product

⟨u|v⟩ = ∑ₖ uₖ* vₖ

The conjugate sits on the first argument and on nothing else. Matrix products act on a state from right to left, so the gate written last in a product is the one applied first.

Units and logarithms

ℏ = 1,   U(t) = e−iHt

A Hamiltonian is measured in angular frequency. Where a physical energy is meant, the units are written out. A logarithm of a probability is taken base two, so an entropy is in bits.

Who it is for

Undergraduate engineering students taking a first course in quantum computing. The only assumed background is linear algebra over the real numbers and enough Python to read a short listing. Every hard word on the page is a technical one, and each is defined where it first appears.
